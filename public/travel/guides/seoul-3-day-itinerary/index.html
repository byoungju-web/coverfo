3 Days in Seoul: A First-Timer's Itinerary

This route covers Seoul's palaces and hanok lanes, a major free museum, Namsan's night views and two modern neighborhoods in three unhurried days. It is arranged around the palaces' weekly closing days so you don't arrive at a locked gate.

By coverfo · Last checked October 31, 2026 · 8 min read

Three days is enough to see the core of Seoul without rushing: the royal palaces and hanok lanes of the old city, a major museum, a view from Namsan, and two very different modern neighborhoods. This itinerary groups sights that sit close together so you spend your time walking around them rather than riding between them. It assumes you are staying somewhere central, such as Myeongdong or Jongno, and using the subway plus the occasional taxi.

Before you go: closing days that shape the plan

Seoul's palaces each close one day a week, and this is the most common way first-timers lose half a day. Arrange your three days around the table below. Hours shown are the winter schedules that apply from November, as listed on the official tourism sites as of October 2026.

If a palace's closing day falls on a public holiday, it usually opens that day and closes the next working day instead. Visitors wearing hanbok, the traditional Korean dress, get free entry to the palaces, which is why you will see rental shops all around Gyeongbokgung.

Tip: The simplest fix is to avoid doing Day 1 on a Tuesday and Day 2 on a Monday. If your dates force it, swap the two days.

Day 1: Gyeongbokgung, Bukchon and Gwangjang Market

Morning: Gyeongbokgung Palace

Start at Gyeongbokgung, the largest of the Joseon-era palaces. Arrive at opening time to walk the courtyards before the crowds build. The Changing of the Royal Guard ceremony takes place in front of Gwanghwamun, the main gate, at 10:00 and 14:00, except on Tuesdays; it may be cancelled in heavy rain or snow. The nearest stops are Gyeongbokgung Station on Line 3 (Exit 5) and Gwanghwamun Station on Line 5. Allow about two hours inside the palace grounds.

Afternoon: Bukchon, Insadong and Ikseon-dong

Walk east to Bukchon Hanok Village, a hillside neighborhood of traditional houses between Gyeongbokgung and Changdeokgung. It is a lived-in residential area, and the busiest section, the so-called Red Zone, is closed to tourists from 17:00 to 10:00. Since March 2025, sightseers found there during those hours can be fined 100,000 won, so visit in the early afternoon. Keep your voice low and do not enter private gates.

From Bukchon, drift downhill to Insadong for craft shops, galleries and traditional tea houses, then continue to Ikseon-dong, a cluster of small hanok alleys now filled with cafes and restaurants. This whole stretch is walkable, with plenty of places to rest.

Evening: Gwangjang Market

End the day at Gwangjang Market, one of Seoul's oldest traditional markets. The food alley stays open until about 23:00 every day, even though the clothing and fabric sections close on Sundays. Try bindae-tteok (crispy mung bean pancakes), mayak gimbap (small seaweed rice rolls), and assorted savory pancakes. The nearest station is Jongno 5-ga on Line 1, Exit 8. Most stalls work on a sit-down, order-and-pay basis, so bring some cash.

Day 2: Changdeokgung, the National Museum and Namsan

Morning: Changdeokgung and the Secret Garden

Changdeokgung is a UNESCO World Heritage Site and many visitors find it more atmospheric than Gyeongbokgung, because its buildings follow the natural slope of the land. The highlight is the Secret Garden (Huwon), a wooded royal garden with pavilions and ponds that you can only enter on a guided tour. English tours run at 10:30, 11:30, 14:30 and 15:30 from March to November; check the official site for winter times. Half the places in each session can be reserved online through the royal palaces' integrated reservation service and half are sold on site, so book ahead if your heart is set on a slot. The garden ticket does not include palace entry, so buy both. The nearest station is Anguk on Line 3, Exit 3.

Afternoon: National Museum of Korea

Take the subway to Ichon Station (Line 4 or the Gyeongui-Jungang Line) and use Exit 2; an underground walkway leads toward the museum. The permanent galleries are free and cover Korean history from prehistoric times through the Joseon era, including celebrated Buddhist sculpture and Goryeo celadon. Special exhibitions charge separately. You could easily spend a whole day here, so pick a few galleries and give yourself two to three hours. On Wednesdays and Saturdays it stays open until 21:00, which makes those good days to swap the order of the afternoon and evening.

Evening: Namsan and N Seoul Tower, then Myeongdong

Head up Namsan for the city lights. You can ride the Namsan Cable Car, or take the Namsan circular bus 01 from Chungmuro Station Exit 2. The grounds around the tower are free to walk; the observatory needs a ticket and is open late every day of the year. Afterwards, come down to Myeongdong for street food and late shopping.

Tip: Clear evenings give the best views from Namsan. Check the forecast in the afternoon and swap Day 2 and Day 3 evenings if the sky looks hazy.

Day 3: Seongsu and Hongdae

Morning and afternoon: Seongsu and Seoul Forest

Spend your last day on modern Seoul. Seongsu, on Subway Line 2, was once a district of factories and handmade shoe workshops. Today its brick warehouses hold cafes, concept stores, K-beauty spaces, pop-up shops and galleries, and the lineup changes constantly. Wander around Seongsu Station and Yeonmujang-gil, then walk to Seoul Forest, a large park that is pleasant for a break.

Evening: Hongdae and Yeonnam-dong

Line 2 runs straight across the city to Hongik University Station. Hongdae is known for buskers, dance performers, independent shops, bars and clubs, and it is at its liveliest after dark. For a calmer dinner, walk north to Yeonnam-dong, where small restaurants and cafes line the streets near the Gyeongui Line Forest Park. If you are flying out the next morning, Hongik University Station is also on the AREX line to Incheon Airport.

Adapting the plan

No itinerary survives every trip unchanged, so here are a few easy adjustments.

  • Rainy day. Move the National Museum of Korea to the wettest day and give it more time; it is large, indoors and free. Note that the guard ceremony at Gwanghwamun may be cancelled in heavy rain or snow.
  • Travelling with children. Shorten the Bukchon walk, which involves steep lanes, and add more time at Seoul Forest on Day 3.
  • Tight budget. Many of the best stops cost little: palace tickets are cheap, the museum's permanent galleries are free, and wearing hanbok gets you into the palaces at no charge.
  • Arriving late on Day 1. Skip the palace, start with Bukchon before 17:00, and head straight to Gwangjang Market for dinner.

Getting around and saving time

  • Transit cards. A T-money card works on subways, buses and many taxis. Seoul also sells a short-term Climate Card for tourists with unlimited rides in Seoul; as of October 2026, VisitKorea lists a 3-day pass at around 10,000 won plus a 3,000 won card. It does not cover the Sinbundang Line or GTX.
  • Taxis. For short hops between Jongno, Myeongdong and Namsan, or late at night, a taxi is often the fastest choice. Our taxi guide covers how to call one with Kakao T.
  • Maps. Use Naver Map or Kakao Map for accurate walking and transit directions. See Korea's essential travel apps for setup tips.
  • Pace. Seoul involves a lot of walking, stairs and hills. Comfortable shoes matter more than an extra stop.

FAQ

Which day is Gyeongbokgung Palace closed?

Gyeongbokgung is closed on Tuesdays. If a Tuesday is a public holiday, it closes the next working day instead.

Do I need to book the Changdeokgung Secret Garden?

Booking is strongly recommended. Entry is by guided tour only, and half of each session's places are reserved online while the rest are sold on site.

Can I visit Bukchon Hanok Village in the evening?

The busiest part of Bukchon is closed to sightseers from 17:00 to 10:00, and violators can be fined 100,000 won. Plan your visit between 10:00 and 17:00.

Is the National Museum of Korea free?

Yes, the permanent exhibition is free. Special exhibitions charge a separate fee.

Is three days enough for Seoul?

Three days covers the main highlights at a comfortable pace. With more time, add a day trip or spend longer in neighborhoods you enjoyed.
