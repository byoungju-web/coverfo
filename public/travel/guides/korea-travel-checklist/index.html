Korea Travel Checklist: Everything to Sort Out Before You Fly

From K-ETA and the new e-Arrival Card to SIM cards, T-money and plug adapters, this checklist covers the practical things first-time visitors to Korea should arrange before departure. Rules are current as of October 2026.

By coverfo · Last checked October 31, 2026 · 8 min read

Most of what makes a first trip to Korea smooth happens before you board the plane: the right entry authorization, a working phone plan, a transport card and a way to pay. This checklist walks through each item in the order you will need it, with the rules as of October 2026. Entry policies in particular change at the end of each year, so confirm anything marked as temporary on the official sites linked below before you fly.

Entry: visa, K-ETA and the e-Arrival Card

Start by checking whether your nationality needs a visa at all. Citizens of many countries can visit Korea visa-free for a short stay, but the allowed length of stay depends on your passport, so check with the Korean embassy or consulate for your country. If you need a visa, you do not need a K-ETA.

K-ETA (Korea Electronic Travel Authorization) is an online pre-travel approval for visa-free visitors. The key facts as of October 2026:

  • Where to apply: only the official website, k-eta.go.kr, or the official K-ETA app. Korea has not appointed any agencies, and look-alike sites have charged many times the real fee.
  • Fee: 10,000 won, paid by card.
  • When: at least 72 hours before departure. Results usually arrive by email, but busy periods can take longer.
  • Validity: three years, or until your passport expires if that comes first.
  • What you need: your passport, an email address, a recent photo and a payment card. Details cannot be edited after you submit, so a mistake means applying again.

Temporary exemption. Travelers from 22 countries and regions are exempt from K-ETA until 31 December 2026: Australia, Austria, Belgium, Canada, Denmark, Finland, France, Germany, Italy, Japan, the Netherlands, New Zealand, Norway, Poland, Singapore, Spain, Sweden, the United Kingdom and the United States (including Guam), plus residents of Hong Kong, Macau and Taiwan. Children aged 17 and under and travelers aged 65 and over are also exempt. If your trip runs into 2027, check k-eta.go.kr for whether the exemption has been extended again.

e-Arrival Card. Korea replaced the paper arrival card with an online version at e-arrivalcard.go.kr, and it has been mandatory for foreign visitors since 1 January 2026. You can submit it up to three days before you land; if you do not arrive within that window, it expires and you file a new one. Holders of a valid K-ETA do not need to submit an e-Arrival Card. That means a traveler from an exempt country who skips K-ETA must fill in the e-Arrival Card instead, while one who applies for K-ETA anyway can skip it.

Phone and internet: SIM, eSIM or roaming

Korea's maps, taxi and delivery apps all assume you have mobile data, so sort this out first. You have four main choices:

  • eSIM: bought online before you leave and installed by scanning a QR code. Check that your phone supports eSIM and is unlocked.
  • Physical SIM (USIM): sold at the carrier roaming centers in the arrivals areas of Incheon, Gimpo, Gimhae and Jeju airports, and at some convenience stores. Stock at convenience stores varies by branch.
  • Portable Wi-Fi ("Wi-Fi egg"): rented at the same airport counters and handy for groups sharing one connection.
  • Home roaming: simplest, but compare the daily price with a local plan before you rely on it.

Free public Wi-Fi is available at airports, subway and train stations and some bus stops. A plan that includes a Korean phone number is useful for some apps and restaurant queues, so check whether yours does.

Money: cards, cash and tax refunds

Korea uses the won (KRW). Major credit and debit cards are widely accepted in shops, restaurants and hotels. Still, carry some cash for traditional markets, street food, small guesthouses and topping up transport cards, which is often easier with cash. Tell your bank you are traveling and check its foreign transaction fees.

Tax refunds. Shops displaying a tax-free sign can refund value-added tax to visitors on purchases of 15,000 won or more. At participating stores you can get an immediate refund at the till by showing your passport: each purchase must be under 1 million won, and immediate refunds are capped at 5 million won in total. Above those limits, or at other stores, you get a refund slip and claim at a downtown refund point or at the airport before departure. Keep goods unused and receipts together, because customs may ask to see them.

Getting around: transport cards and apps

Buy a T-money card (or a compatible transit card) on arrival. It is sold at airport and city convenience stores and in subway stations, and you top it up at convenience store counters or station machines. One card covers subways and buses in Seoul and many other cities, and some taxis and convenience stores accept it too; check locally before relying on it outside the big cities. Some cards are sold empty, so load money before your first ride. Seoul also sells short-term unlimited transit passes for tourists under the Climate Card name, which can be worth it if you plan many rides in a few days.

Download the apps before you land. The core set is a Korean map app (Naver Map or Kakao Map), Kakao T for taxis, the Korail app for trains, a translation app and, if you want takeaway, a delivery app. Our guide to Korea's essential travel apps explains each one, and the taxi guide covers hailing and paying for cabs as a foreigner.

Install Naver Map or Kakao Map and switch it to English before you leave home. Both use Korean address and transit data, so routes and bus times tend to be more complete than in apps you use at home.

Power, plugs and the rest of your bag

Korea runs on 220 volts at 60 hertz, and wall sockets take plugs with two round pins (the same style used in much of continental Europe, often labeled Type C or F). Phone and laptop chargers usually handle 100 to 240 volts, so they only need a plug adapter; check the label on hair dryers and straighteners before plugging them in. Adapters are sold at airports, convenience stores and duty-free shops, and many hotels lend them at the front desk.

  • Driving: if you plan to rent a car, bring your home licence plus an International Driving Permit issued under the Geneva or Vienna convention. See our Jeju road trip guide for rental details.
  • Insurance: travel insurance that covers medical treatment and trip changes is worth having.
  • Comfort: comfortable walking shoes, a light layer for air-conditioned trains and, in winter, warm clothing for cold, dry air.

FAQ

Do I need a K-ETA to visit Korea in 2026?

If you visit visa-free you need one, unless you are from one of the 22 temporarily exempt countries and regions, which are exempt until 31 December 2026. Children 17 and under and adults 65 and over are also exempt.

How much does K-ETA cost and how long is it valid?

The official fee is 10,000 won, and approval is valid for three years or until your passport expires. Apply only on k-eta.go.kr or the official app, at least 72 hours before departure.

Do I still need to fill in an arrival card?

Yes, unless you hold an approved K-ETA. Since 1 January 2026 the arrival card is online only and can be submitted up to three days before arrival at e-arrivalcard.go.kr.

What plug adapter do I need for Korea?

Korea uses 220V, 60Hz power with two round-pin sockets, the common European style. Most phone and laptop chargers only need a plug adapter, but check single-voltage appliances.

Can I get a tax refund in the shop?

At participating tax-free shops you can get an immediate refund with your passport on purchases of at least 15,000 won and under 1 million won each, up to 5 million won in total.

Can I drive in Korea with my home licence?

You need your home licence together with an International Driving Permit issued under the Geneva or Vienna convention, valid for one year from entry.
